**The objection, at its strongest:**

Everything said in favor of the Catholic Church — apostolic succession, sacramental worship, an unbroken line to the Fathers, veneration of Mary and the saints — is equally true of the Eastern Orthodox. They too trace their bishops to the apostles; they too have valid sacraments; they too preserve the faith of the first millennium. If anything, the Orthodox claim to have kept that faith *unchanged*, while Rome added novelties — the papal supremacy, the *Filioque*, later dogmas. So the "four marks" argument does not point uniquely to Rome. It points at least as well to Orthodoxy, and perhaps better.

**The response:**

This objection deserves more respect than most, because it is largely *right about what it affirms*.
The Orthodox Churches are not a Reformation-style break.
They have true apostolic succession, valid sacraments, a real Eucharist, and a deep and ancient holiness.
The Catholic Church herself teaches this: she regards the Orthodox as true particular Churches, calls their sacraments genuine, and speaks of the division between East and West as a wound in a communion that was once whole — not as the East simply ceasing to be Church.
So the question is not "which side is Christian?" but "where is the *fullness* of the Church Christ founded, and what does its unity require?"

The decisive issue is the one the objection raises: **visible unity, and the office that secures it.**
Christ prayed "that they may be one," and He gave Peter a distinct role — the keys, the charge to strengthen the brethren, the office of steward over the household.
The Orthodox preserve nearly everything the Catholic Church does *except* a permanent, visible principle of unity for the whole Church.
And the consequence is telling: without such an office, Orthodoxy has no agreed way to convene a binding council or settle a dispute for the entire communion.
It has held no ecumenical council in over a thousand years, and its jurisdictions have at times broken communion with one another over questions no one had authority to resolve.
This is precisely the fragmentation the Petrine office exists to prevent.

On the specific "novelties" charged against Rome, the picture is not East-pure versus West-innovating.

1. **Papal primacy** is not a medieval invention but the continuation of the role the New Testament gives Peter and the early centuries gave Rome — the see to which disputes were referred and whose judgment carried unique weight, acknowledged in the East as well as the West for the first thousand years.

2. **The *Filioque*** (that the Spirit proceeds "from the Father *and the Son*") is a genuine and serious theological difference, but both traditions confess the same underlying faith in the Trinity; the Catholic Church has clarified that it does not intend two sources of the Spirit, and much of the dispute is over the authority to add a clarifying phrase, not over the reality confessed.

3. **Later dogmas** (such as the Immaculate Conception) are not additions *to* the deposit of faith but drawings-out *of* what was always held — the same kind of development the East also underwent in its own councils.

None of this diminishes what the Orthodox have kept, and a Catholic should say so gladly.
The claim is not that Orthodoxy is false, but that it is a communion missing one thing Christ provided — the visible center of unity — and that the marks of the Church, held *together and in full*, still converge most completely on the Church in communion with Peter's successor.
The East and West are two lungs of one body; the argument is simply about which has kept breathing with both.
