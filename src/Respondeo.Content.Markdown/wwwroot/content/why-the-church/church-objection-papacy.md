**The objection, at its strongest:**

"You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church" does not establish a papacy. The "rock" is Peter's *faith* — his confession that Jesus is the Christ — not Peter himself. Even if Peter had a special role, nothing shows it was an *office* meant to pass to successors, still less to the bishops of Rome. The papacy is a later institution read back into a single verse.

**The response:**

The objection rightly presses on the text, so start there.
The claim that the "rock" is only Peter's faith and not Peter runs against the grain of Jesus' own words.
He does not say "on this faith"; He renames *the man* — Simon becomes *Peter*, "Rock" — and in the same breath hands *him* the keys and the power to bind and loose.
Renaming someone in Scripture (Abram to Abraham, Jacob to Israel) marks a change in that person's role, not an abstract principle.
In the original setting the wordplay is unmistakable: "You are Rock, and on this rock I will build."
The most natural reading is the one the objection resists — Jesus builds on the man He has just renamed.

The surrounding image seals it.
"The keys of the kingdom" deliberately echoes Isaiah 22, where a single steward is given "the key of the house of David" — a royal *office*, with authority to open and shut, held by one man on the king's behalf and passed on when he falls.
Jesus is not describing a private grace to Peter; He is installing a steward over His kingdom.
And a steward's office, by its nature, outlives the man who holds it — that is what makes it an office rather than a favor.

Does the New Testament then treat Peter as first among the apostles?
Consistently.
He is always named first in the lists, speaks for the Twelve, is the first to preach at Pentecost, is singled out to "strengthen your brethren," and settles the decisive question at the Council of Jerusalem.
The pattern is too steady to be accidental.

That the office would continue is not read *back* into the text; it is required by what the Church is *for*.
Christ built a Church to last "to the end of the age," and the need for a visible point of unity does not die with Peter any more than the need for the other apostles' authority died with them — which is why *all* the apostles appointed successors.
The early Church bears this out: from the first centuries, disputes were referred to Rome, and Rome's bishop intervened in other churches' affairs with an authority others acknowledged.
The papacy is not a verse stretched into an institution; it is the office Christ gave Peter, carried on for the same reason every apostolic office was carried on — because the Church He founded was built to endure.
