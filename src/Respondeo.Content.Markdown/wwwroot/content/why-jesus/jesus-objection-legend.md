**The objection, at its strongest:**

The "Liar, Lunatic, or Lord" trilemma quietly omits a fourth option — *Legend*. Perhaps the historical Jesus was a Jewish teacher who never claimed divinity, and his followers exalted him only gradually, so that the high claims in the Gospels reflect later belief rather than his own words.

**The response:**

The legend theory has one fatal problem: time.
Legends that transform a man into a god need generations to grow, long enough for the eyewitnesses who could object to have died out.
Here that window never opens.

Paul's letters — undisputed even by skeptical scholars — are written roughly twenty to twenty-five years after the crucifixion, and they already treat Jesus as divine.
More striking still, Paul quotes creeds and hymns (as in Philippians 2 and 1 Corinthians 15) that are older than his letters, carrying a high view of Christ back to within a few years of the events.
The exalted claim is not the endpoint of a long evolution; it is present at the very beginning.

The claim is also *embedded*, not tacked on.
Strip the divine self-understanding out of the Gospels and the narratives fall apart — the trial charge, the reaction of His opponents, the worship He receives all lose their sense.
A late legend can be peeled away; this cannot, because it is woven through the earliest layer of the tradition.

Finally, the exaltation happened among **monotheistic Jews** — the people least likely of all to start worshipping a man.
For first-century Jews to attribute divinity to a crucified Galilean is not the natural drift of legend; it is a shocking rupture that demands an explanation strong enough to overturn their deepest conviction.
That the belief arose *anyway*, that early, and among *those* people, is far harder to explain as gradual invention than as their response to what they claimed to have witnessed.
