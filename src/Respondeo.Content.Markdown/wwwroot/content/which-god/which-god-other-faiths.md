> "That which is known of God is manifest in them. For God hath manifested it unto them." &mdash; Romans 1:19

Reason has reached one personal God who can reveal Himself, marked by credible signs. But that description fits several of the world's great claimants, at least in part. Before naming the one who speaks, it helps to see clearly **how the God reason reaches relates to — and differs from — the major alternatives.** Each holds a genuine fragment; the question is which one keeps the whole.

**Deism** rightly holds that reason reaches a single Creator. But it then locks Him out of His own creation — a watchmaker who winds the world and walks away. This contradicts the very step we just took: a *personal* God, a mind and a will, is exactly the kind of being who *could* and plausibly *would* engage His creatures. Deism affirms the Creator but denies the possibility of the encounter — it stops one step short.

**Islam** shares much: one God, unlimited, personal, the Creator who has spoken. On divine unity and transcendence it stands close to the God of the philosophers. The parting of ways is not over *whether* God is one, but over *who* He has finally shown Himself to be — and over the reading of the same covenant history that runs through Abraham.

**Eastern conceptions** — the impersonal Absolute of some strands of Hindu thought, or the Buddhist reticence about a creator God — often reach toward the same unlimited ground of being reason discloses. But they tend to make that ground **impersonal** — a state, a principle, a ground to be dissolved into rather than a Someone to be known. That is precisely the fork the previous step turned on: reason's first cause is a knower and a lover, not a blind Absolute.

**A note on tone.** Naming these differences is not dismissal. Each tradition preserves something true — the Creator's oneness, His transcendence, the reality of an ultimate ground. The claim here is narrower: that only one of these keeps *every* thread reason has traced — one, personal, and self-revealing in verifiable history.

## See it today: many maps of one mountain

Travelers describe the same mountain from different trailheads: one sees the sheer cliff, another the gentle meadow slope, a third only the cloud-wrapped summit. None is simply lying, and each map is genuinely useful. But they are not equally complete, and only one route actually reaches the peak.

The world's God-ward traditions are like these partial maps. The task is not to pretend the maps are identical, nor to sneer at the incomplete ones, but to ask which one charts the whole climb — including the final stretch the others leave blank.

**Where this leads.** Weighing the claimants keeps returning us to one covenant story — a particular God who acts in a particular history, with a particular people. So the last question of this stage is the sharpest: of all who claim to speak, why the God of **Israel** — the God of Abraham, the prophets, and the Shema?
