The Eucharistic Miracle of Lanciano is among the oldest and best known of the reported Eucharistic
miracles of the Catholic Church, often described as the first on record. According to the tradition it
occurred in the city of Lanciano, in the Abruzzo region of Italy, around the 8th century, and the
relics it left behind are still venerated there today.

## What happened

A Basilian monk was celebrating Mass at the monastery of St. Longinus, troubled by doubts about the
Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist. As he pronounced the words of consecration, the host is said
to have visibly changed into living flesh and the wine into blood, which soon coagulated into five
globules of differing shapes and sizes. Because no contemporary record survives, even the monk's name
is unknown; the earliest written accounts date from the later medieval period. The relics were
preserved through the centuries and today rest in the Church of San Francesco in Lanciano, displayed in
a silver and glass reliquary made in Naples in 1713.

## The scientific findings

In 1971 the relics were subjected to scientific examination led by Dr. Odoardo Linoli, a professor of
anatomy, histology, and pathology, assisted by Dr. Ruggero Bertelli. The reported findings were
striking:

- The flesh is **human cardiac tissue**, identified as a cross-section of the wall of a heart
  (myocardium), complete with its characteristic structures.
- Both the flesh and the blood are **human**, and both share the same blood type, **AB**.
- The blood was said to contain the proteins and minerals of normal fresh blood, in roughly the
  proportions found in a living body.
- No trace of **preservatives or artificial agents** was found, which is notable given the material's
  age and its centuries of unprotected exposure.

These claims are widely repeated in Catholic literature and were summarized in later studies, though
they have not been subject to independent replication, and some historians question the tradition on
account of the gap between the alleged event and its first written records. They are reported here as
findings held in devotion, not as settled scientific fact.

## How the Church regards it

Lanciano predates the modern canonical processes for judging alleged miracles, so it is venerated by
long tradition rather than by a formal modern decree. Pilgrims and popes have honored the relics for
centuries; Pope John Paul II recalled venerating them as a cardinal. The Church does not require belief
in any particular reported miracle; such events are offered as aids to faith, not as articles of it.

## Why it matters

Whatever one concludes about the physical relics, the account illustrates the doctrine it points to:
the Catholic belief that, at the consecration, the bread and wine truly become the Body and Blood of
Christ. It is telling that the tradition remembers the miracle as an answer to a priest's *doubt* about
that very teaching. The miracle is a sign pointing to the doctrine, not the basis for it.
