Lourdes, in southern France, is the site of the 1858 apparitions of the Virgin Mary to St. Bernadette
Soubirous, and has since become one of the most visited pilgrimage shrines in the world, drawing
several million pilgrims each year and known especially for reported healings.

## What happened

Over the eighteen apparitions of 1858, Bernadette was directed by the Lady to a spot in the grotto of
Massabielle where, after she scratched at the ground, a spring emerged. Pilgrims soon began bathing in
and drinking the water, and reports of unexpected cures followed. In the decades since, many thousands
of claimed healings have been brought to the shrine.

## How the cures are examined

Uniquely among shrines, Lourdes maintains a rigorous and deliberately skeptical process, open to
physicians of any faith or none. A claimed cure is first studied by the Lourdes Medical Bureau, which
gathers the medical record and tests whether the healing was sudden, complete, lasting, and without
medical explanation. Cases that survive this scrutiny are referred to the International Medical
Committee of Lourdes for further review. Even then, the medical finding is deliberately modest: the
committee certifies only that a cure is *medically unexplained*. Whether to call it miraculous is a
separate judgment, reserved to the healed person's own bishop.

That division of labor is the key to understanding the claim. The doctors do not pronounce on the
supernatural, and the Church does not ask them to; medicine says only what it can honestly say, that it
has no explanation, and the theological judgment is made elsewhere, on its own grounds.

## The results

Millions have come and a great number of cures have been claimed, yet after this severe filtering the
Church has formally recognized only around 70 as miraculous. The small proportion is itself part of the
point: the process is built to weed out the ordinary, the psychosomatic, the misdiagnosed, and the
merely premature, so that what remains has already passed a demanding test.

## Why it matters

Lourdes offers a striking example of the Church subjecting reported healings to independent medical
scrutiny rather than assuming them. The recognized cures are presented as signs of God's mercy, while
the shrine's deeper work is quieter and larger: the consolation of the sick and suffering who come in
their millions, most of whom are not cured in body and yet return home changed.
