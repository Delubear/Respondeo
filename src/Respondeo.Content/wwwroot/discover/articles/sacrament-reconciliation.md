**What it is.**
In Reconciliation — also called Confession or Penance — the risen Christ exercises the power He gave His apostles: "Whose sins you forgive are forgiven them."
The penitent confesses their sins to a priest, expresses sorrow, and receives absolution — Christ's own forgiveness, spoken aloud, with certainty.

**Why celebrate it.**
This is the sacrament that reopens the door for anyone who has wandered.
It exists not to shame but to free.
Where a private "I'm sorry" leaves us wondering whether we are truly forgiven, Confession gives the answer out loud: *you are forgiven*, on the authority of Christ Himself.
It restores the grace lost through serious sin and heals the smaller wounds of daily failure.
This is why it is the natural first step home — not a hurdle to clear, but the mercy the sacrament was made to give.

## Why not just tell God directly?

You can, and you should — every act of contrition is addressed to God. But the question assumes that God prefers to work without visible means, when in fact he consistently chooses to work through them. He could have fed the crowd invisibly; instead he multiplied bread through the Apostles' hands. He could forgive without water; instead he gave Baptism. Confession follows the same pattern of the Incarnation: grace reaching us through something we can see and hear.

There is also a human reason. Sin spoken aloud loses its power to hide. Naming a fault to another person — under the absolute seal of the confessional — is humbling in a way that private regret is not, and the words *I absolve you* answer the doubt that private prayer can leave unhealed: *am I really forgiven?*

## Where the sacrament comes from

On the evening of the Resurrection, Jesus breathed on the Apostles and said: *Receive the Holy Spirit. Whose sins you forgive are forgiven them; whose sins you retain are retained* (John 20:22–23). To forgive or retain, the Apostles had to hear what was done — which presumes confession. The Church has understood these words, from the earliest centuries, as the institution of a sacrament of forgiveness entrusted to the ministers of the Church, not merely a general encouragement to be merciful.

## What actually happens

The priest is not the source of forgiveness; God is. The priest acts *in persona Christi* — in the person of Christ — as the instrument through whom Christ's own absolution is given. What the sacrament requires of the penitent is simple: contrition (real sorrow), confession (an honest naming of sins), and a purpose of amendment (the intention to change), followed by the penance given.

## How to make a good confession

If it has been a long time, the priest expects that and will help. A simple form: begin with the Sign of the Cross and *Bless me, Father, for I have sinned; it has been [time] since my last confession.* Then confess your sins plainly, listen to any counsel, pray an Act of Contrition, and receive absolution. That is the whole of it — and on the far side is the relief the sacrament exists to give.
