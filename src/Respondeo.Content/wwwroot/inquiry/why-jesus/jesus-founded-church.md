If the Resurrection vindicates Jesus' authority, a practical question follows at once: how was that authority meant to reach us?
Jesus wrote no book and left no constitution.
What He left was a *community* — a body of people with named leaders and a commission.
So the question is not merely historical curiosity; it is about where His authority is to be found after the Ascension.

The claim of this step is deliberately modest: not yet *which* Church is His, 
but that He founded a **visible** one at all — a society with structure and teaching authority, not merely a scattering of individual believers.

**The case that Jesus founded a visible Church:**

1. **He gathered and organized, He did not merely inspire.**
   He chose twelve, an unmistakable echo of the twelve tribes — the reconstitution of God's people.
   He trained them, gave them a leader, and sent them out with His own authority: "whoever hears you hears me."

2. **He entrusted a teaching office, not just a message.**
   To Peter He gives the keys and the charge to strengthen the others; to the Twelve the power to bind and loose, 
   to forgive, to baptize and teach all nations.
   These are grants of *ongoing authority*, meant to outlast the men who first received them.

3. **He promised permanence.**
   "The gates of hell shall not prevail against it," and "I am with you always, to the end of the age."
   A movement expected to last until the end of time needs a form that can carry it there — a visible, continuing body, not a passing enthusiasm.

4. **This is exactly what the first generation understood.**
   The earliest believers did not scatter into private spiritualities; they formed a structured community with apostles, 
   elders, sacraments, and councils that settled disputes with authority.
   The Church we see in the historical record is the one Jesus is described as founding.

## See it today: what actually outlasts a founder

A feeling dies with the person who felt it; a movement rarely survives the generation that started it.
The one thing that reliably carries a founder's purpose across centuries is an institution — named successors, settled roles, 
a body that can act when the founder is gone.
Memories fade; offices endure.

Jesus built for exactly that endurance — leaders, a commission, the power to teach in His name.
That tells us what to look for: if He truly founded such a body, 
it would need to be the kind of thing that could still be standing two thousand years later.
A passing enthusiasm could not; only an institution founded to outlast its founder could even be a candidate.

**Where this leads.** If the risen Christ founded a visible Church and gave it authority to teach in His name, then a final, unavoidable question opens up.
That Church was meant to endure and to be *recognizable*.
So where is it to be found today?
