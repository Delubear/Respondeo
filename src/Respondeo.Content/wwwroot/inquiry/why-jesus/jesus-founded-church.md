If the Resurrection vindicates Jesus' authority, a practical question follows at once: how was that authority meant to reach us?
Jesus wrote no book and left no constitution.
What He left was a *community* — a body of people with named leaders and a commission.
So the question is not merely historical curiosity; it is about where His authority is to be found after the Ascension.

Notice how the Resurrection narratives end.
The risen Christ does not simply appear, reassure His friends, and depart.
He gathers them, singles out leaders, and sends them out with His own authority: "whoever hears you hears me." 
Whatever else He meant to leave behind, He plainly meant to leave a *body* — people charged to carry His word, not merely remember it.

That single observation is enough to close this stage.
It does not yet settle *what kind* of body, how it was structured, or whether it was meant to endure for centuries — those are the questions the next stage takes up directly.
It settles only this much: the One whose authority the Resurrection vindicates did not leave that authority homeless.
He entrusted it to a community He Himself assembled.

## See it today: the shape of what He left

A teacher who only wanted to be remembered leaves behind admirers.
A founder who wants his purpose carried on leaves behind people with jobs — someone to lead, someone to speak in his name, a group bound together by a task.
The difference is visible in what gets left behind: sentiment on one side, structure on the other.

What Jesus left looks unmistakably like the second kind.
Named leaders, a commission, delegated authority — the raw materials of an institution, not just a memory.

**Where this leads.** The risen Christ handed His authority to a community rather than a book.
That turns the next stage into one long, unavoidable question: did that community amount to a *Church* in the 
full sense — a visible society meant to endure and to be recognized — and if so, where is it to be found today?
