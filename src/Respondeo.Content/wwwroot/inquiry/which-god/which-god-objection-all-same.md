title: "Don't All Religions Point to the Same God?"
summary: Isn't it arrogant to single out one tradition, when every faith is really reaching for the same divine reality?
---

**The objection, at its strongest:**

1. Every great tradition reaches toward an ultimate reality — Brahman, the Tao, Allah, the God of the philosophers, the God of Israel.
2. These are just different cultural names and images for the *same* transcendent truth, glimpsed from different angles.
3. To insist that one of them is uniquely correct is parochial arrogance — the accident of where you happened to be born, dressed up as certainty.

**In response:**

- **Agreeing partly is not agreeing entirely.** That the traditions *overlap* — many affirm a transcendent source, a moral order, a call to humility — is real and worth honoring. But overlap is not identity. They make *contradictory* claims about the very thing at issue: whether the ultimate is personal or impersonal, whether it has acted in history or stands aloof, whether it can be known or only dissolved into. These cannot all be true at once, and pretending they are does not respect the traditions — it flattens them.
- **The "same mountain" picture smuggles in its own dogma.** To say "all paths climb the one mountain" is itself a specific, contested claim about ultimate reality — the view from a supposed summit above all the traditions. It is not neutral ground; it is one more position, and it quietly contradicts every tradition that says the peak actually matters. The pluralist has not escaped the question "which is true?" — only answered it in favor of his own account.
- **The decisive fork is personal versus impersonal.** This stage already turned on it: reason reaches a first cause that *knows and loves*, not a blind Absolute. A tradition that dissolves the personal God into an impersonal ground is not describing the same reality from another angle; it is denying the very feature the argument established. The disagreement is about the thing itself, not the vocabulary.
- **Where you were born bears on *belief*, not on *truth*.** That your upbringing shaped what you find plausible is true of the pluralist too — and of the atheist, and of everyone. It is a fact about how convictions form, not a verdict on which convictions are correct. The honest question is never "who was raised where?" but "which account actually holds together and meets the evidence?"

So the point is not that other traditions are worthless — this stage has insisted they each keep something true. It is that only one keeps *every* thread reason traced: one, personal, and self-revealing in history. Singling it out is not arrogance if it is where the argument leads; it would be arrogance to override the argument to keep the peace.
