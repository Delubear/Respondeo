> Essence answers to what a thing is, while existence, or being, answers to that it is, or the fact that it exists.
>
> &mdash; The Thomistic Institute

Alongside act and potency, a second distinction helps us understand Aquinas's reasoning about God: **essence** and **existence**.
Aquinas made their relationship central to his account of why creatures depend on God.

**The puzzle of contingent being.**
You can know *what* something is without knowing *whether* it exists. You know what a unicorn or a phoenix is, yet neither one is real.
Now think of a dog: knowing what a dog is still doesn't tell you whether there is one in the room.
What it is and whether it exists are different questions. Aquinas follows that distinction beyond our ideas to the dependence of real things.

**The distinction, in plain terms:**

- **Essence:** *what* a thing is: the kind of thing it is, its nature or definition. The essence of a dog is to be a certain kind of living animal.
- **Existence:** *that* a thing is: the sheer act of being, the fact that the thing is actually there rather than merely possible.

A thing's *nature* is what makes it the kind of thing it is, including its characteristic powers and activities.
A dog can grow, sense, and move about because it is a living animal; a stone cannot.
Here, *nature* means what something is, not the outdoors.

These aren't two objects sitting side by side, as though a dog were made from a nature plus a separate piece called existence.
The distinction is between the kind of being the dog is and its actually being there at all.

**Existence is received, not owned.**
Aquinas reasons that whatever belongs to a thing must follow from its nature or come from a cause.
But a creature's nature cannot cause its own existence: to do that, it would already have to exist.
Nor does being a dog mean existing necessarily; dogs come into being and pass away.
So the dog's existence is something its nature receives, not something that nature produces for itself.

This is why Aquinas speaks of being as something a creature *has*, not something it *is*.
Its nature does not give it existence; it depends on a cause for actually being there.
Here is the principle Aquinas draws from this:

> Everything, then, which is such that its act of existing is other than its nature must needs have its act of existing from something else.
>
> &mdash; St. Thomas Aquinas, *De Ente et Essentia*, ch. 4

The dog does not account for its own being; its existence is derived, dependent, borrowed at every moment.

**Why this matters for the Five Ways.**
This is a related argument Aquinas develops in *De Ente et Essentia*, not simply another name for the Third Way:

1. A creature's nature does not account for its existence.
2. What receives existence depends on a cause; it cannot bring itself into being.
3. If every cause gives existence only by receiving it, the dependence is never explained by adding more such causes.
4. Aquinas therefore concludes to a source that does not receive existence: its **essence just is existence**.

Think of the difference between something that passes a gift along and the source of the gift.
Passing it through more hands doesn't explain where it comes from.
Likewise, more causes that receive existence cannot replace a source that does not need existence given to it.

*This being, whose very nature is to exist, Aquinas identifies with God.*

You don't have to master this distinction before reading the Ways.
It helps explain what Aquinas means when he later describes God as **Being Itself**, rather than one more dependent thing.
