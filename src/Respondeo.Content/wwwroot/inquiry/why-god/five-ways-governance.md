summary: "Why do things without minds act toward definite outcomes?"
---

> We see that things which lack intelligence, such as natural bodies, act for an end.
>
> &mdash; [*Summa Theologiae* I, Q. 2, A. 3](summa/prima-q002#article-3)

The earlier ways traced *change* and *causal dependence*. The Fifth Way notices something different: **directedness**. 
Things that have no minds of their own nevertheless behave *as if aimed* &mdash; consistently, and toward outcomes that benefit them.

An **end** here does not mean a conscious goal; it means a *result aimed at*: a definite outcome that a thing reliably tends to produce. 
A seed tends toward becoming a plant, not a puddle. Fire tends toward heating, not freezing.

Aquinas's point is that **aiming requires an aimer**:

> Whatever lacks intelligence cannot move towards an end, unless it be directed by some being endowed with knowledge and intelligence; as the arrow is shot to its mark by the archer. 
>
> &mdash; [*Summa Theologiae* I, Q. 2, A. 3](summa/prima-q002#article-3)

**The argument, step by step:**

1. Natural things that lack knowledge act toward an end: they achieve their goal not by chance but nearly always in the same way, so as to obtain the best result.
2. What lacks knowledge cannot tend toward an end unless directed by something that *has* knowledge and intelligence.
3. Mindless striving that reliably hits a target must be aimed by an intellect that can hold that target in view.
4. Therefore some intelligent being exists by whom all natural things are ordered to their end &mdash; *and this being we call God.*

**A clarification.** This is not the modern "argument from design" that points to complexity and infers a tinkering engineer. 
Aquinas points to something more basic and harder to escape: the plain fact that unknowing things are *directed at all* &mdash; 
that causes reliably point toward specific effects rather than nothing in particular. 
Even the regularities physics depends on are instances of this directedness.

## See it today: a plant on the windowsill

Watch a houseplant for a week and you will see the Fifth Way unfold &mdash; not as a story about the distant past, but as directedness happening *now*.

Consider everything the plant does, none of which it *understands*:

- Its **roots grow downward** and toward water, though the seed has no map of the soil.
- Its **leaves turn to face the light** (phototropism), tracking the sun across the day.
- Its **stomata open and close** to balance moisture and gas exchange.
- **Photosynthesis** converts light, water, and air into sugar: a chemistry aimed with precision at the plant's nourishment.
- Its cells **divide, specialize, and repair** in an ordered sequence toward a mature, reproducing organism.

Notice two things at once. First, the plant has **no awareness** of any of this: it does not know what water is, or light, or sugar. 
Second, its parts and processes are unmistakably **aimed**: each reliably tends toward the plant's flourishing, "nearly always in the same way, so as to obtain the best result."

**Doesn't biology explain this?** Yes: biology explains how plants respond to light and water, and evolution helps explain the development of their capacities.
The argument does not require those explanations to fail.
Nor does it require chance to be unreal. Random events can occur within stable natural processes, and natural selection is not simply random scattering.

Aquinas asks about the **directed powers** these explanations describe.
The plant responds as it does because of its nature; its chemistry has characteristic effects rather than producing anything whatsoever.
Even a probabilistic process has definite possible outcomes and a characteristic pattern.
Explaining one tendency through another can deepen our understanding, but it still leaves a world of tendencies to explain.

**Why does Aquinas infer intelligence?** The seed's growth is ordered toward a mature plant before that plant is there.
The future plant cannot reach backward and guide the seed, and the seed cannot picture what it is becoming.
Its directedness belongs to its nature. But why does that nature order these processes toward this outcome?

Aquinas argues that something entirely without knowledge cannot itself originate the ordering of means to an end.
An intellect can grasp an outcome before it exists and order causes toward it.
The plant *reaches* for the light because its nature is aimed &mdash; and a nature aimed without knowledge of its own points beyond itself to a **directing intelligence**.
This does not make the plant a puppet: its own nature is the means by which it grows toward the light.

**Where this leads.** The Fifth Way is not looking for a missing biological mechanism or an unusually clever engineer inside the universe.
It asks why natural causes have their definite orientation toward effects at all.
Aquinas concludes to an intelligence ordering nature as a whole &mdash; and this is what he calls God.
