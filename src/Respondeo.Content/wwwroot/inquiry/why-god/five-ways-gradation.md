> Among beings there are some more and some less good, true, noble and the like.
>
> &mdash; [*Summa Theologiae* I, Q. 2, A. 3](summa/prima-q002#article-3)

The earlier ways traced *change*, *causal dependence*, and *contingency*. The Fourth Way begins from something we do every day without noticing: we **rank** things. 
We call one act kinder than another, one proof more rigorous, one landscape more beautiful. These *more* and *less* judgments are the starting point.

Aquinas's claim is that **comparison presupposes a standard**:

> *More* and *less* are predicated of different things according as they resemble in their different ways something which is the maximum. 
>
> &mdash; [*Summa Theologiae* I, Q. 2, A. 3](summa/prima-q002#article-3)

**The argument, step by step:**

1. We find degrees of goodness, truth, and other perfections in things.
2. Aquinas considers these in relation to a maximum: a fullness of being and goodness that limited things resemble in different ways.
3. He argues that things possessing being and goodness in a limited, received way depend on a source for what they have.
4. If a cause also receives its being and goodness, it cannot be the ultimate source. The explanation must reach what does not receive them from another.
5. This source is not merely the best item in a collection: it is the fullness of being and goodness, and the cause of these in other things &mdash; *and this we call God.*

**A clarification &mdash; this is the hardest of the five.** A few points help make the argument clearer:

- **What is a perfection?** Here, a *perfection* is a real good or fulfillment, not flawless performance. Health is a perfection of a living thing; understanding is a perfection of a mind.
  A tree and a person can both be good, though in different ways, because each can possess the goods that fulfill its nature.
- **Is Aquinas just measuring things?** He is interested in **being, goodness, and truth**, not simply quantities like height in inches.
  Comparing two numbers does not prove there is a largest number, and comparing temperatures does not prove there is a hottest object.
  Ranking things is the starting point, not the whole argument.
- **What does *participation* mean?** A thing participates in a perfection when it has it without being that perfection itself.
  A good friend is truly good, but is not goodness itself: your friend has a human life and human goodness, with their own limits.
  Aquinas argues that creatures receive their being, and with it the goodness belonging to them. He follows that dependence toward a source that does not receive being or goodness.

Aquinas uses fire as an example of a maximum causing heat; that reflects medieval natural philosophy, not modern physics.
His wider question is about the source of the being and goodness things receive, as the [essence and existence](node/aquinas-five-ways?section=five-ways-essence-existence) section explores.

## See it today: the warmth of a room

You do not need a philosophy library to begin: notice how you already speak.

Step into a warm room and you naturally say it is **warmer** than the hallway, **cooler** than the oven.
Now notice the heater. The room is really warm, but its warmth comes from another source.
Comparing temperatures and explaining where the warmth comes from are different questions.
The heater helps us picture the second question: how can something have a quality it does not produce for itself?

Now shift from heat to the perfections Aquinas actually means:

- You call one friend's act **kinder** than another's. Both may be good, but neither act exhausts what goodness means.
- You call one explanation **truer** than another. Each can help you understand, but neither contains every truth there is to know.
- You see a healthy tree flourish. Its roots, leaves, and growth are good because they fulfill its nature as a living thing; a damaged tree may lack some of that health.

Here is the pivot. In each case the things you notice **have** a good &mdash; kindness, understanding, health &mdash; but they do not have every good without limit.
Aquinas asks what accounts for the being and goodness they do have.
His answer is not simply that anything partial needs a better example beside it. Rather, creatures receive their existence and the perfections that belong to them.
Like the room whose warmth comes from a heater, they have something they do not give themselves.

**Where this leads.** The other ways ascend through chains of *causes*; the Fourth Way begins with a ladder of *goodness and being*.
Aquinas follows the dependence of things that possess these goods toward a source that does not have to receive them.
That fullness of being and goodness itself, from which every lesser and partial good derives, is what we call God.
