> We find in nature things that are possible to be and not to be, since they are found to be generated, and to corrupt.
>
> &mdash; [*Summa Theologiae* I, Q. 2, A. 3](summa/prima-q002#article-3)

The earlier ways traced *change* and *causal dependence*. The Third Way looks at the most basic fact of all: that things **exist at all**, when they need not have. 
A tree, a star, you &mdash; each is **contingent**: able either to be or not to be. 
It came to be, and it will pass away. Its existence is not guaranteed by what it is; it is *received*.

Aquinas asks what such fragile, borrowed existence ultimately depends on.

**Aquinas's argument, step by step:**

1. Things exist that come into being and pass away: they can be or not be.
2. Aquinas reasons that if everything could cease to be, at some time nothing would have existed. This is the disputed step we will examine below.
3. But from nothing, nothing comes &mdash; so if there had ever been nothing, nothing would exist now. Yet things plainly exist.
4. Therefore not everything is like these perishable things; there must be something **necessary**.
5. A necessary being either has its necessity from another or not.
6. An order of received necessity cannot explain itself entirely through further receivers, so Aquinas concludes to a being **necessary of itself** &mdash; which he identifies with God.

**Why must there be something necessary?** Aquinas reasons that if everything could cease to exist, there would once have been nothing.
That step raises a question: even if each thing can pass away, why couldn't things overlap, with new things coming to be as others disappear?

The later question is different: even if something always existed, would it exist **through itself**, or receive its necessity from another?
A chain of receivers, however long, still leaves that question unanswered.

A related approach asks about the dependence of things that **receive existence**.
The [Essence & Existence](node/aquinas-five-ways?section=five-ways-essence-existence) section develops that reasoning.
The candle below gives us a familiar starting point.

## See it today: the flame of a candle

A candle flame is one of the clearest pictures of contingency you can watch.

The flame exists right now &mdash; but notice how it exists. It is not a fixed object; it is a **continuous event**, sustained moment by moment:

- It burns only while **wax is drawn up the wick** and vaporized *now*.
- The vapor ignites only while **oxygen reaches it** *now*.
- The whole flame persists only while **heat feeds back** to melt the next wax *now*.

Cut off any of these &mdash; pinch the wick, cover the flame, remove the air &mdash; and it does not linger. It simply **stops being**. 
The flame never *owns* its existence; it holds it on lease, renewed instant by instant, and depends entirely on conditions it does not supply.

Now widen the lens. The candle itself was made, and it will be consumed.
The intuition resists here: a candle flame obviously depends on something else, but a **rock** or a **planet** seems simply to *sit there*, self-standing.
Yet duration is not the same as self-sufficiency.
A boulder retains its structure through the interactions of its atoms; a planet holds together through gravity.
Their stability is real, but it is not independence. Lasting a billion years does not make something the source of its own existence.

Here the related argument from essence and existence takes us further.
Being a rock, a planet, or a candle does not account for actually existing: none brings itself into being or exists simply by being that kind of thing.
Aquinas argues that such things receive existence from a cause.
If that cause also receives existence, it too depends on another.
Adding more receivers extends the dependence; it does not supply a source that exists without receiving existence.
The explanation must therefore reach something whose existence is not borrowed &mdash; something that does not merely *have* existence, but *is* existence.

**"Well, what caused God?"** If this source needed to receive existence from another, it would be one more borrower, not the ultimate source.
The conclusion is not one more candle-flame needing to be lit and kept lit, but a being whose very nature is to exist.
That is why Aquinas does not place God among the things whose existence needs to be given to them.

**Where this leads.** The Third Way seeks something **necessary of itself**, not merely something very old or long-lasting.
The related argument from dependence leads toward a source whose existence is unreceived and unfailing.
