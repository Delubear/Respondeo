Welcome.

Whatever brought you here, whether it be curiosity, doubt, hope, or a restlessness you can't quite name, you've arrived to ask one question:

***Is there a God?***

Pause for a moment over how large that question really is.
It is not one question among many: it is the question beneath all the others.
Its answer changes how we understand everything else: why anything exists, what kind of world we inhabit, and whether our lives reach beyond death.
It also opens further questions about love, justice, and what we are here for.
Few questions can reshape a whole life. This is one of them.

And notice what it means that you are asking it at all.
Maybe you feel a yearning &mdash; a sense that there is more than what you can touch and measure, 
and that you were made for something your daily life keeps hinting at but never quite delivers.
Or maybe you simply feel lost, and the question rose up on its own, unbidden, because some part of you refuses to stop searching for the answer.
Or perhaps you don't feel any of that. You may simply want to know whether belief in God makes sense.

You don't need a particular feeling to begin. The question is worth asking wherever you find yourself.

You are free to go slowly, to sit with the question, and to let it stay open a while. 
The Catholic tradition does not hurry you past that wonder; it meets you right where it begins,
not by demanding faith, but by offering reason, and trusting that an honest mind can be led gently toward what is real.

Following St. Thomas Aquinas, we hold that God's existence can be **demonstrated** from things you already observe: change, 
cause and effect, and the plain fact that anything exists at all.
These are not appeals to feeling. They are arguments you can examine, test, and push back on, and you are invited to do exactly that.

The claim we will weigh together is simple: the world around us does not explain itself.
Aquinas argues that following its dependence all the way leads us to God. We'll look at why and whether each step holds.

You don't have to settle the question before beginning. You can examine an argument one step at a time and see where it leads.

The Thomistic approach we'll take respects your caution:
- It does not ask you to *assume* God to get started.
- It asks only that you follow the evidence of change and existence wherever it leads.
- You don't have to agree with an argument before examining it.
- If a step seems wrong or unclear, that is the place to stop and look more closely.
- The aim is not merely to introduce an interesting possibility, but to show why Aquinas concludes that God really exists.
- If the argument succeeds, it requires honest attention, because it is not a matter of opinion or preference: it is a matter of what is true.


The only thing asked of you is a willingness to follow the evidence.
