The Eucharistic Miracle of Lanciano is among the oldest and best known of the reported Eucharistic
miracles of the Catholic Church. According to the tradition, it occurred in the city of Lanciano,
Italy, around the 8th century.

## What happened

A Basilian monk, celebrating Mass, was troubled by doubts about the Real Presence of Christ in the
Eucharist. As he pronounced the words of consecration, the host is said to have visibly changed into
flesh and the wine into blood. The relics were preserved and have been venerated in Lanciano ever
since.

## The scientific findings

In 1971 the relics were subjected to scientific examination led by Dr. Odoardo Linoli, a professor of
anatomy and pathology. The reported findings were that the flesh is human cardiac tissue and the
blood is human blood of type AB, with no sign of preservatives. These claims are widely repeated in
Catholic literature, though they have not been the subject of independent replication.

## How the Church regards it

Lanciano predates the modern canonical processes for judging alleged miracles, so it is venerated by
long tradition rather than by a formal modern decree. The Church does not require belief in any
particular reported miracle; such events are offered as aids to faith, not as articles of it.

## Why it matters

Whatever one concludes about the physical relics, the account illustrates the doctrine it points to:
the Catholic belief that, at the consecration, the bread and wine truly become the Body and Blood of
Christ. The miracle is a sign pointing to that teaching, not the basis for it.
